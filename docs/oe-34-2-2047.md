## Mosaic-based waveguide lenses designed by the adjoint method based on the finite-element scheme

TAKESHI FUJISAWA, 1,* YASUHIDE TSUJI, 2 TAKUYA MITARAI, 3 YUSUKE SAWADA, 3 TAKUYA OKIMOTO, 3 TAKUO HIRATANI, 3 KENTO KOMATSU, 3 HIDEKI YAGI, 3 AND NAOKI FUJIWARA 3

3 Transmission Devices Laboratory, Sumitomo Electric Industries, Ltd., Japan * fujisawa@hosei.ac.jp

Abstract: Waveguide lenses based on mosaic structure are proposed and experimentally demonstrated. The devices are designed by an adjoint method based on the finite-element method (AM-FEM). In AM-FEM, it is easy to calculate the gradient of the objective function with respect to the refractive index distribution, and it is especially useful for multi-port and multi-mode problems. First, to demonstrate the effectiveness of the AM-FEM, simple 1 × 2 power splitters are designed, and the importance of the initial structure is demonstrated together with experimental results. And then, the design of single-mode waveguide lenses is demonstrated. After passing through the mosaic lens, the wavefront of the light is aligned, and the light can traverse a slab region, which has no confinement mechanism in the transverse direction. Also, multimode waveguide lenses are presented for the first time, showing low-loss and almost wavelength-independent characteristics. The designed devices are fabricated, and the measured results are in good agreement with the theory. These devices can be useful for various wavefront controlling devices.

## 1. Introduction

The demand for increased data transmission capacity for AI and cloud applications requires optical communication within and between data centers. To increase the transmission capacity, miniaturization of optical components is required to install more optical pluggable transceivers within the same footprint. Furthermore, new integration technologies for optical semiconductor devices, such as Co-Packaged Optics (CPO), are sought as one approach to realizing an optoelectronic integration in next-generation networks [1]. Optical devices using Si waveguides are suitable for these integrated components. They are well suited for these integrated components and are considered highly appropriate for compact, integrated device architectures.

A waveguide lens is a kind of waveguide crossing, which is composed of input/output waveguides and a slab region between them, and they have been realized in silica [12,13] and Si waveguides [14]. In these studies, waveguide widths are tapered or modulated to align the phase of the light after the input waveguide. Thanks to the aligned phase, the light can pass through the

Alot of passive waveguide components based on Si waveguides [2-8] have been investigated so far, such as low-loss waveguide [2], spot-size converter [3], mode/wavelength multiplexers [4-9], and waveguide crossings [10]. The sizes of these devices can be reduced by 1/100 compared with those of compound semiconductor waveguides due to the large refractive index difference ( ∆ ) between the core and cladding. For example, the footprint of wavelength multiplexers based on Mach-Zehnder interferometers based on Si waveguides is smaller than 1/100 than that of III-V semiconductor ones [11].

slab region, which has no confinement in the lateral direction. However, the width modulated Si waveguide lenses have relatively large insertion losses and wavelength dependence (about -1 ∼ -3 dB from 1500 to 1600 nm [14]).

In this paper, waveguide lenses based on mosaic structures designed by AM-FEM are proposed and experimentally demonstrated. In the AM-FEM, it is easy to calculate the gradient of objective function with respect to the refractive index distribution, and is, especially, useful for muti-port and multi-mode problems. First, to demonstrate the effectiveness of the AM-FEM, simple 1 × 2 power splitters are designed, and the importance of the initial structure is demonstrated together with experimental results. And then, the design of single-mode waveguide lenses is demonstrated. After passing through the mosaic lens, the wavefront of the light is aligned, and the light can traverse a slab region, which has no confinement mechanism in the transverse direction. Two-mode waveguide lenses are also designed and fabricated. For both single- and two-mode lenses, low-loss and almost wavelength independent characteristics are obtained. The designed devices are fabricated, and measured results are in good agreement with the theory. These devices are useful for wavefront controlling devices.

Mosaic-based devices have attracted a lot of attention recently, due to their ultrasmall size and light controllability [15-27]. They are composed of input/output waveguides and a Si plate between them. The Si plate is divided into pixels, and 1 (Si) or 0 (cladding) is assigned to each pixel. The size of the pixels is about 100 nm square, which can be fabricated, for telecommunication wavelength. The mosaic patterns are designed by so-called direct binary search (DBS) method. However, conventional DBS method requires huge computational time. Recently, an adjoint method was introduced to design these mosaic-based devices [28-30]. In the adjoint method [31-34], the gradient of the objective function with respect to the refractive index distribution is calculated, and the total index distribution is renewed with the gradients. It should be noted that the topology optimization usually optimizes the geometry of the device with a continuous shape. Therefore, in conventional topology optimization, the gap, whose size cannot be fabricated, easily occurs without a constraint. In the adjoint method for mosaic-based devices [28-30], the manufacturable pixel size is set from the beginning. In these methods, finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) [28,29] or finite-element method (FEM) [30] were used for the electromagnetic solver. Especially, the adjoint method based on FEM (AM-FEM) is useful, since the gradient for each pixel can be obtained by simply differentiating the FEM matrix. Since the same FEM matrix can be used for different input/output conditions, it enables more efficient design for multi-port and multi-mode problems compared to the adjoint method based on FDTD. However, experimental demonstrations of devices designed by AM-FEM has not been done.

## 2. Theory

## 2.1. Frequency domain FEM

Figure 1 shows the schematic of a mosaic-based device considered here. It consists of input/output waveguides and a mosaic region between them. The mosaic region is divided into square pixels, as shown in Fig. 1. The relative permittivity of the pixel ( i , j ) is denoted as ε r,ij hereafter. We used frequency-domain FEM [35,36], in which arbitrary waveguide modes can be launched from the input waveguide, and it can calculate a complex scattering coefficient S , for desired input/output waveguides. We consider equivalent two-dimensional ( xz ) structure by using an effective index approximation. In the FEM, the entire electromagnetic field can be calculated by solving following simultaneous equation, 

$[P ]\{\Phi \}= \{\Psi \}$

where [ P ] is the finite-element matrix, { Ψ } is the input field, and { Φ } is the total field distribution ( E y for TE, and H y for TM modes). [ P ] is given by [19.20],

$[P ]= \sum\nolimits_e {\int\!\!\!\int {({p\{{{N_x}} \}{{\{{{N_x}} \}}^T} + p\{{{N_z}} \}{{\{{{N_z}} \}}^T} - k_0^2q\{N \}{{\{N \}}^T}} )dxdz} }$

Fig. 1. A schematic of mosaic-based device.

<!-- image -->

where the subscript e denotes an element, k 0 is the free-space wavenumber. p and q are 1 and n 2 for TE modes (1/ n 2 and 1 for TM modes), where n is the refractive index. { N } is the shape function vector and details can be found in [19,20]. From (1) and (2), in the FEM, the information on the material index is only included in [ P ]. Also, once we solve (1) by using LU factorization, the field solution { Φ } can be easily calculated for different input conditions { Ψ } , since [ P ] is not changed. This feature is very useful for multi-mode and multi-port problems. The scattering coefficient is given by

${S_{out,in}} = {\{{{g_{out}}} \}^T}\{\Phi \}$

where in and out are input and output port · mode number. g out is the eigen mode vector of out .

## 2.2. AM-FEM

In AM-FEM, the gradients of the fitness function with respect to the permittivity is calculated and the index distribution is renewed based on the gradients. In mosaic-based devices, the pixel is 1 or 0. However, to differentiate the permittivity distribution, the permittivity of ( i , j ) pixel is expressed by a continuous value as

${\varepsilon _{r,ij}}(\rho )= {\varepsilon _{clad}} + ({{\varepsilon_{core}} - {\varepsilon_{clad}}} )H(\rho )$

where ε core and ε clad are permittivity of core and cladding, respectively. H is given by

$H(\rho )= \left\{ {\begin{array}{c} {0.5{{({2\rho } )}^m}\quad 0 \le \rho \le 0.5}\\ {1 - 0.5{{({2 - 2\rho } )}^m}\quad 0.5 \le \rho \le 1} \end{array}} \right.$

⎩

where ρ is 0 to 1 and m is an adjustment parameter. If m is infinity, H becomes step (Heaviside) function. Figure 2 shows H ( ρ ) for various values of m . For small m , the value of H ( ρ ) is smoothly changed from 0 to 1 with ρ . For large m , H ( ρ ) is sharply changed around ρ = 0.5, and for m = 64, H ( ρ ) is almost binarized. By expressing the permittivity by (4), we can differentiate it by ρ as

$\frac{{\partial {\varepsilon _r}}}{{\partial \rho }} = \left\{ {\begin{array}{c} {m{{({2\rho } )}^{m - 1}}({{\varepsilon_{core}} - {\varepsilon_{clad}}} )\quad 0 \le \rho \le 0.5}\\ {m{{({2 - 2\rho } )}^{m - 1}}({{\varepsilon_{core}} - {\varepsilon_{clad}}} )\quad 0.5 \le \rho \le 1} \end{array}} \right.$

⎩

The fitness function, F , is defined to include the scattering coefficient of the device. If there is one input and output waveguides, as shown in Fig. 1, the target for the optimization should be a function of S 11 (reflection) and S 21 (transmission). When only power is considered, F is a function of the absolute values of the scattering coefficients as F ( | S 11 | , | S 21 | ).

We consider minimizing F in the optimization. By differentiating (1) by the permittivity of ( i , j ) pixel, we obtain following equation.

$\frac{{\partial [P ]}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}}\{\Phi \}+ [P ]\frac{{\partial \{\Phi \}}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}} = \{0 \}$

Note that, { Ψ } in (1) does not depend on the permittivity distribution. By multiplying [ P ] -1 to (7) from the left, we obtain

$\frac{{\partial \{\Phi \}}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}} ={-} {[P ]^{ - 1}}\frac{{\partial [P ]}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}}\{\Phi \}$

Differentiating the scattering coefficients (3) gives

$\frac{{\partial {S_{out,in}}}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}} = {\{{{g_{out}}} \}^T}\frac{{\partial \{\Phi \}}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}} ={-} {\{{{g_{out}}} \}^T}{[P ]^{ - 1}}\frac{{\partial [P ]}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}}\{\Phi \}={-} {\{{{\lambda_{out}}} \}^T}\frac{{\partial [P ]}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}}\{\Phi \}$

$\{{{\lambda_{out}}} \}= {({{{[P ]}^{ - 1}}} )^T}\{{{g_{out}}} \}$

where { λ out } is referred to as the adjoint vector. When differentiating the absolute values of the scattering coefficients, the following equation holds.

$\frac{{\partial |{{S_{out,in}}} |}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}} = \frac{1}{{2|{{S_{out,in}}} |}}\frac{{\partial {{|{{S_{out,in}}} |}^2}}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}} = \textrm{Re} \left[ { - \frac{{S_{out,in}^\ast }}{{|{{S_{out,in}}} |}}{{\{{{\lambda_{out}}} \}}^T}\frac{{\partial [P ]}}{{\partial {\varepsilon_{r,ij}}}}\{\Phi \}} \right]$

The gradient of the objective function, F ( | S 11 | , | S 21 | ), with respect to the permittivity of ( i , j ) pixel is given by

$\frac{{\partial F}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}} = \frac{{\partial F}}{{\partial |{{S_{11}}} |}}\frac{{\partial |{{S_{11}}} |}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}} + \frac{{\partial F}}{{\partial |{{S_{21}}} |}}\frac{{\partial |{{S_{21}}} |}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}} = \sum\limits_{n = 1}^2 {\frac{{\partial F}}{{\partial |{{S_{n1}}} |}}\frac{{\partial |{{S_{n1}}} |}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}}}$

Substituting (11) into (12) yields

$\frac{{\partial F}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}} = \sum\limits_{n = 1}^2 {\textrm{Re} \left[ { - \frac{{\partial F}}{{\partial |{{S_{n1}}} |}}\frac{{S_{n1}^\ast }}{{|{{S_{n1}}} |}}{{\{{{\lambda_{out}}} \}}^T}\frac{{\partial [P ]}}{{\partial {\varepsilon_{r,ij}}}}\{\Phi \}} \right]}$

Once we obtain the gradient of F , we can update the permittivity distribution by

$\varepsilon _{r,ij}^{M + 1} = \varepsilon _{r,ij}^M - \alpha \frac{{\partial F}}{{\partial {\varepsilon _{r,ij}}}}$

where α is an adjustment parameter. Practically, ρ distribution is updated instead of ε r .

## 3. Design of mosaic-based power splitters based on AM-FEM

Here, the designs of mosaic-based 1 × 2 power splitters (PSs) are demonstrated to show the usefulness of AM-FEM. The left panel of Fig. 3 shows the schematic of the PS. The device is based on Si waveguide with the thickness of 220 nm. It consists of one input and two output waveguides. Between input and output waveguides, there is a rectangular mosaic region. The size of mosaic region is L x = L z = 2.4 µm. The region is divided into 24 × 24 = 576 square pixels, whose side length is 100 nm. The widths of the input and output waveguides are w in = w out = 0.6 µm. The output waveguide spacing is d = 0.8 µm. The target output power in the lower output waveguide is γ . Fitness function is defined as

$FOM = \frac{1}{{{N_\lambda }}}\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{{N_\lambda }} {\{{|{\gamma - {T_{low}}({{\lambda_i}} )} |+ |{({1 - \gamma } )- {T_{up}}({{\lambda_i}} )} |} \}}$

where T low and T up are transmission powers to lower and upper waveguides at wavelength λ i . N λ is the number of wavelengths. Here, 1530, 1550, and 1570 nm are considered for the optimization. In the gradient-based optimization, the choice of the initial structure is very important. ## 4. Waveguide lenses based on mosaic structure

Figure 7 shows the schematic of waveguide lens based on mosaic structure. It consists of one input/output waveguide, whose widths are w in and w out . There is a slab region with the longitudinal length of L slab , where there is no confinement in the transverse direction. Between slab and input/output waveguide, a mosaic region is placed, as shown in Fig. 7. The region is divided into square pixels with a side length of 100 nm. By properly designing the mosaic region, the transverse phase of the light is adjusted, and can traverse the slab region with small loss. We call this device as 'waveguide lens', in this paper. In previous studies, the waveguide lenses were demonstrated by width-modulated tapers [13,14]. However, as stated in Introduction, for Si-waveguide, the loss and the wavelength dependence were relatively large. Here, by using the mosaic structure, the loss is reduced and nearly wavelength independent characteristics are obtained.

## 4.1. Single-mode lens

First, we consider a single-mode lens. We set w in = w out = 0.4 µm, and L x = L z = 2.0 µm. Therefore, the input-side mosaic region has 20 × 20 = 400 pixels. We impose xz -symmetry to the mosaic region. The output-side mosaic region is z -inversion of the input-side pattern. The fitness function is defined as

$FOM = \frac{1}{{{N_\lambda }}}\sum\limits_{i = 1}^{{N_\lambda }} {|{1 - {T_{out}}({{\lambda_i}} )} |}$

where T out is the output power. Wavelengths of 1530, 1550, and 1570 nm are considered. L slab is set to 2.0 µm. The left panel of Fig. 8 shows the FOM as a function of iterations for three initial structures. We used the same initial structure as in the case of 1by2 PS, namely, ρ = 0.498, 0.7, and 0.9 in all the pixels. Parameter m is increased from 2 to 64. For all cases, the FOMs are converged with only 50 iterations, and FOMs of ρ = 0.498 and 0.9 are minimum and maximum, as in the case of PS. The right panel shows the binarized ρ distribution of the optimized pattern. To show the whole structure, input and output mosaic region, together with slab region are shown. As in the PS, by increasing the value of initial ρ , Si region (white) is greatly increased. For ρ = 0.7 and 0.9, the mosaic regions are almost Si. Figure 9 shows the calculated transmission spectra of bulk (all pixels are Si), ρ = 0.498, 0.7, and 0.9 for L slab = 2, 4, 6, and 8 µm. For the bulk, since there is no phase adjustment function, the loss is monotonically increased for larger values of L slab . The losses are -3 and -6 dB for L slab = 2 and 8 µm around 1550 nm. Corresponding field distributions are shown in the left panel of Fig. 10. Light from input waveguide is broadened like a 2-D spherical wave due to increased width of the mosaic region and is leaked into the transverse directions of the slab region, leading to the loss. For ρ = 0.498, 0.7, and 0.9, the AM-FEM design is carried out for L slab = 2 µm and we investigate the transmission by changing L slab with the same mosaic pattern. For ρ = 0.498 (second panel of Fig. 9 and 10), the loss for optimized L slab (2 µm) is almost zero, showing the superiority of the mosaic structure for manipulating the wavefront. However, the loss is increased for larger values of L slab . This is because the pattern for ρ = 0.498 is only effective for L slab = 2µm. For ρ = 0.7, and 0.9 (the third and fourth panels of Fig. 9 and 10), the transmission losses are very low for L slab = 2 µm although the loss value is slightly increased compared with that of ρ = 0.498. Interestingly, the loss is not so changed for larger values of L slab . As shown in the field distributions, the wavefront after the input-side mosaic region is more aligned, and light can traverse the slab region for longer L slab . By changing the width and length of the mosaic region, low-loss transmission for longer L slab may be possible.

There is a possibility that since a 2-D optimization using the effective index approximation was done, the transmission is degraded for a full 3-D structure. Figure 11 shows the calculated transmission spectra of the bulk and the waveguide lens for ρ = 0.498 by using 2-D FEM and 3-D FDTD. The results are largely consistent, although the loss is slightly increased in the 3-D calculation. Therefore, for the design of the waveguide lenses, the use of 2-D approximation is reasonable. ## 5. Conclusion

We proposed and experimentally demonstrated waveguide lenses based on mosaic structures. AM-FEM is used for the design of the devices, and the number of iterations can be greatly reduced compared with that of conventional DBS method. The designed single-mode lenses have characteristics of low-loss ( &lt; -1 dB) and almost wavelength independent spectra, which is superior to that of waveguide-width modulated waveguide lens [14]. For some initial structures, low-loss transmission is possible for longer slab lengths, showing the flexibility of the mosaic structure for wavefront engineering. Two-mode lenses were also designed and demonstrated, having low-loss and small wavelength dependence spectra. These waveguide lenses based on mosaic structures are useful for various applications for manipulating wavefront of the guided wave light.
